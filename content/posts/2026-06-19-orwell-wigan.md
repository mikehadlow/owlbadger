This is the second book I’ve read for my “month of George Orwell” and also the
second non-fiction book that Orwell published. It was published in 1937 and
gives an account of working class conditions in the Midlands at the time.
Orwell lived in the area from January to March 1936, lodging with, visiting,
and talking to various poor households. He was commissioned to write it by
Victor Gollancz of the Left Book Club. The book consists of two parts; the
first is a very journalistic account of his journey and the conditions and
people he encounters, the second consists of a long discussion of why socialism
is not as popular as he thinks it should be.

The first half starts with his description of a lodging house run by the
Brooker family and the various characters who inhabit it. He describes the
squalid conditions and blames these on the ignorance and narrow mindedness of
the Brookers. He thinks that even with their difficult economic circumstances,
their laziness and lack of care make the conditions in the house far worse than
they could be. He doesn’t say where the place is, other than “the industrial
north”. Orwell goes down a mine and describes the work of the miners. It sounds
horrendous. Cramped tunnels deep underground, full of dust and dangerous gas.
Hard physical labour for seven hours a day. What a life! He goes on to describe
the awful housing conditions of the working class in northern cities,
describing a succession of awful hovels he visited. Tiny rooms, crammed with
beds. Everywhere overcrowded. No running water or sewage, filth everywhere.

Orwell discusses the unemployment figures of mid 30s, he suggests that they are
misleading and that the true numbers affected are multiples of the headline
numbers. He discusses whether the unemployment benefit and PAC (Public
Assistance Committee) payments are sufficient. He also talks about the working
class diet and complains that they often choose poorly: white bread, lots of
sugar, condensed milk; and that this contributes to malnutrition. 

Part two opens with a discussion of the British class system. Orwell famously
describes himself as lower-upper-middle-class. So finely grained. I’m not sure
anyone could do that today. He points out that the middle class is a cultural
as well as a financial class, with a different accent, and different prejudices
and beliefs. He discusses whether the working class smells bad and concludes
that they do! Of course most poor houses didn’t have running water, so it was
difficult to have a full-body bath, and the most that working people could
manage was a weekly soak in shared water.

He asks why socialism hasn’t emerged in Britain, despite it being, in his mind,
an obviously better system. He thinks its lack of appeal to the working class
is its culturally middle-class proponents, the academic and inaccessible
language it’s often couched in, and the eccentric crankiness of many of its
adherents. He talks about his own political journey, from snobbish public
schoolboy, to Imperial policeman, to an avowed enemy of oppression, both
imperial and domestic. He says he tried to understand the working class by
becoming a tramp, but then admits that they as little represent the working
class as the literary establishment represents the middle.

There is a long polemic decrying technology and mechanisation; the “machine
age” as he calls it. He thinks many socialists’ obsession with
industrialisation is a reason for their unexpected (in his eyes) unpopularity.
He makes several sweeping assumptions; that the profit motive and capitalism is
the reason that poverty exists, and that society should make a choice to slow
or stop technological progress. He thinks that culture is cheapened by
industrial society and people eat less well because of it. In many ways he’s
similar to J.B. Priestley in this respect, but Priestley has the humility to
understand the counter argument, and makes the point that people weren’t just
poor, they frequently starved to death in the pre-industrial era. Orwell is on
stronger ground when he criticises socialists for decrying everything
bourgeois. He thinks it’s pointless harassing people for their middle class
tastes, when it’s more important to persuade them of the economic and practical
benefits of socialism. Disliking them for who they are is counter productive in
his opinion. 

I would have liked Orwell to be more specific in what he means by “socialism”
and “capitalism”; which particular characteristics he wants us to keep or
abandon. He says “democratic socialism” frequently, which must imply that it’s
something people would choose, but does he want a revolution? He rails against
technological progress and the profit motive, so one assumes his ideal world is
some kind of agrarian collectivism?

Orwell is an excellent writer. Direct and unornamented, but exceptionally good
at capturing an image and an atmosphere. He makes the reader fully understand
the hopelessness and squalor of working-class life during the depression,
especially among the unemployed. The book, and Orwell, are often credited with
winning the 1945 general election for Labour. It seems he very much succeeded
in his aim of bringing the hardships of the poor to the attention of the
public, so in that it must be considered a success. For me, I found the second
half polemic fascinating as a critique of the comfortably off middle-class
left-wing; much of it could be said of the same kinds of people a century
later. He apparently upset a lot of communists and socialists with this book,
but nowhere near as much as he would with Animal Farm and 1984. One thing you
can be sure of with Orwell, he’ll never write what he thinks will make him
popular; he just doesn’t care who he upsets. That’s what makes him such an
undisputed great of the 1930s.
