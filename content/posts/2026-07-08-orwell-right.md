This is the fourth volume of the Folio Society’s Reportage box set of George
Orwell’s non-fiction works. The first three volumes are his three non-fiction
books: Down and Out in Paris and London, The Road to Wigan Pier, and Homage to
Catalonia, the final two, of which this is the first, are collections of
essays. At the time of writing this review I’ve already started D.J. Taylor’s
biography Orwell, so I’ll wait until I review that to describe the man himself, but
needless to say Orwell is one of, if not the, most influential political
writers of the 20th century. This volume of essays includes some famous pieces,
one of which The Lion and the Unicorn is almost a mini-book in itself. I’ll
review the essays that interested me the most one by one rather than talking
about the book as a whole.

A Hanging and Shooting an Elephant both describe events that occurred during
his five years in Burma as an imperial policeman. A Hanging is unusually
non-Orwellian in style, a rather baroque, but quite excellent description of
hanging a Burmese man. It’s quite hideous in its mundane facts; the way the
man meekly walks to the scaffold, the grimy courtyard of the gallows. Orwell
finds himself horrified by the act of taking another human life. Shooting an
Elephant is similarly damning of colonial authority. He describes his hatred of
the Burmese and how they hated him in turn, while at the same time having to
play the authoritarian administrator and kill a now harmless elephant. It’s
interesting that, while Orwell was bitterly opposed to British imperialism, it
never became his core political project. Despite his 5 years in Burma he was
always more interested in the plight of the British poor rather than the much
larger number of imperial subjects. I suppose he thought that the onset of
socialism in the UK would inevitably lead to independence for the empire?

Hop Picking and Clink both describe his adventures amongst the London and Home
Counties poor. Hop Picking tries an as-told-by dumbed down style which lacks
authenticity and comes across as rather patronising. But the essay is a good
description of what Hop Picking must have been like in the thirties. It was
seen as a poor family’s summer holiday, which goes to show how grim life must
have been back in London, if ten-hour, back-breaking work on a farm counted
as a rest! Also fascinating that it’s in the area of Kent where I grew up. He
travels through Ide Hill and Sevenoaks before landing a job on a farm near
Maidstone. Clink is written in the more familiar journalistic Orwell style of
Wigan Pier and Catalonia. A funny description of Orwell attempting to get
himself into prison and failing thanks to the light-touch of the British
criminal justice system. Heartening how friendly the 1930s bobbies are.

Bookshop Memories is a classic case of being careful about what you wish for,
as Orwell finds his love of books turning to disdain when faced with them by
the thousand. His short essay on Why I Joined the Independent Labour Party
reveals the kind of socialism Orwell preferred. He disdained the incremental
cautious tinkering favoured by the Parliamentary Labour Party, preferring the
all-out revolutionary overthrow of private-property and capitalism espoused by
the ILP. He was only a member for a short while, falling out with them at the
beginning of WWII because of their anti-war stance. The essay My Country Right
or Left is a condemnation of conscientious objectors. Orwell thinks the fight
against Nazi Germany more important than any principled objection to war. Also
included is a review of Hitler’s Mein Kampf, where he points out that Hitler
transparently listed his totalitarian objectives in print and that there is no
question that he should be resisted.

I enjoyed the unexpected long essay on Boys’ Weeklies. These were cheap
magazines with titles like Gem and Magnet that featured ongoing serials about
public schoolboys or tales of derring-do in the empire and during WWI. They
were hugely popular, oddly among poorer boys as well as the public schoolboys
themselves. Orwell thinks they are establishment propaganda and wonders if a
socialist boys’ magazine would be possible - it wasn’t it seems. Fascinating
how the cheap magazine was such a ubiquitous feature of the thirties with
titles covering almost every niche interest.

Another long essay, Inside the Whale, is about the state of British literature
between the wars. Orwell was a great admirer of Henry Miller’s Tropic of
Cancer, which is the cornerstone of the essay. Orwell strikes a very familiar
pose to any modern Guardian reader when he writes:

> What is quite obviously happening, war, or no war (WWII had just broken out),
> is the break-up of laissez-faire capitalism and of the liberal-Christian
> culture. Until recently the full implications of this were not foreseen. Almost
> certainly we are moving into an age of totalitarian dictatorships.

Such progressive opinions echo away down the decades, and still today, almost a
century later we have people like columnist George Monbiot constantly talking
of “late stage capitalism”, as if capitalism is in terminal decline. Yet if
Orwell could have time travelled 80 years into the future he would have found
laissez-faire capitalism alive and thriving, and liberal-Christian culture (in
the broad sense) still the dominant world ideology. The lesson is obviously
never to make predictions - especially about the end of capitalism!

The Lion and the Unicorn takes up perhaps a quarter of the volume. He wrote it
in 1940 when Britain stood alone against Nazi Germany. He starts the
essay with a long paean to the British character and the island itself. Orwell
is both intensely patriotic and anti-fascist, but also convinced that British
capitalism was not only doomed but responsible for the defeats that had led to
the country’s present peril. He insists that the only way Britain can win
against Germany and Italy is by instigating a socialist revolution and
installing a government which takes full control of the economy. He thinks
capitalism is doomed, simply because he is convinced a planned economy is more
efficient. I suppose to some extent he was vindicated, first by the centralised
government control of war production, even if it didn’t take actual ownership
of the companies concerned, and later by the landslide Labour victory in 1945
which led to widespread nationalisation. In the long term of course he was
wrong; capitalism survived and flourished and the nationalisation was rolled
back in the 1980s.

Orwell is iconoclastic, always clear in his opinions. He writes brilliantly and
persuasively. It’s no wonder that he stands as such a giant of political
thought. Of course with the hindsight of nearly a century, it’s easy to see
where he misses the mark, but at the time he must have appeared prophetic. No
wonder he was so admired and hated in equal measure. His refusal to toe the
line of any party or ideology but instead work from first-principles of truth
and human decency mark him out as a truly great man.
