This book was a recent purchase, bought only a couple of weeks before reading.
After being deep in Orwell-land for the last month, I felt I had to read a
biography. This was recommended in various places as one of the best. I knew
nothing of D.J. Taylor before ordering this book, but he is apparently a
well-respected journalist, novelist, and writer. He’s a self-confessed Orwell
obsessive, coming back repeatedly to the same subject. This book is a 2019
rewrite of his original 2003 biography. Several new collections of
correspondence, particularly between Orwell and various girlfriends, have come
to light in the intervening years, shedding new light on Orwell the
man, and particularly his romantic life.

The book opens with Orwell’s family, childhood, and much hated prep school in
Eastbourne. Orwell’s health problems go back to his very earliest childhood,
but otherwise he seems as though he was a smart gifted boy. Many of his
contemporaries say that he was unfairly hard on his school; that it was the
usual mixed bag, not as unremittingly awful as Orwell paints it. No hint of his
later socialism appears at this stage in his life; he seemed a typical product
of the British upper middle class, even writing patriotic poems during WWI. But
what is clear is that, even as a schoolboy, he was independent-minded and had a
strong anti-authoritarian streak. He went on to Eton after winning a
scholarship, which he seemed to have enjoyed, although he did the bare minimum
of effort once there. Taylor also relates Orwell’s relationship with Jacintha
Buddicom, his childhood sweetheart, whose book, Eric and Us, is apparently one
of the best sources on Orwell’s childhood. Their relationship ended when Orwell
tried to force himself on her during a walk in the countryside, after which
they didn’t speak until shortly before his death. It seems she had no idea that
the famous author George Orwell was her childhood friend Eric Blair, until it
was pointed out by a friend.

Orwell decided not to go on to university, probably because of lack of funds,
and instead secured a post with the imperial police in Burma. He had Burmese
connections on his mother’s side; his maternal grandfather was a French
merchant who had settled there. Taylor complains that it is hard to find much
about Orwell’s time in Burma. Orwell himself only left two short essays, A
Hanging, and Shooting an Elephant, and some short stories, later collected as
Burma Days. How much of himself
is in any of these is hard to know. Taylor speculates about Orwell’s sex life
in Burma. It was quite common for the British administrators to take Burmese
mistresses and use the services of prostitutes, but we have no proof that
Orwell did the same, although as Taylor points out, the author of Burmese Days
seems to know plenty about both. On his return to the UK Orwell intended to
propose to Jacintha Buddicom, but she was in hiding having a baby by another
man, so he assumed he’d been rejected. It seems it was at this point that
Orwell decided to become a writer, much to the horror of his parents.

After a spell living with his parents in Southwold, Suffolk, Orwell moved to
Paris. He was a quarter French and fluent in the language, so he found it far
easier to integrate into working class Paris than many of his fellow expatriate
authors. Unlike most of those other 1920s writers, he didn’t mix in the
literary scene and seems to have associated primarily with poor Parisians. He
had a relationship with a prostitute named Suzette until she ran off with his
money, an episode that notably doesn’t appear in his book about the period,
Down and Out in Paris and London. Back in the UK Orwell again lived mostly with
his parents in Southwold, living as a rather eccentric member of the small
middle class community there. He pursued several love affairs, none very
successfully. It was also at this time that he made several forays into the
London underworld, where he posed as a tramp. Down and Out portrays this as a
continuous period of extreme poverty, but in reality he collected the material
for the book on shorter forays and returned to comfortable middle-class life in
between.

After the publication of Down and Out Orwell worked for various 3rd division
private schools in West London. He wrote Burmese Days and A Clergyman’s Daughter
during this time. Again there were unsuccessful courtships, Brenda Salkeld and
Eleanor Jaques being the subjects of this period. I suppose at that time he
wasn’t much of a catch; an awkward tall lanky man who had given up a well paid
imperial job to live with his parents and pursue a, so far, profitless career
as a writer. In 1934–35 Orwell worked at Booklovers’ Corner, a second-hand
bookshop on the border of Hampstead and Camden. This period was the subject of
his third novel Keep the Aspidistra Flying. During this time he was introduced
to the literary society of London and made many new friends including his
future wife Eileen O'Shaughnessy. At last a successful courtship!

In 1936, Orwell gave up the job at Booklovers’ Corner to take up a commission
from Victor Gollancz, a left-wing publisher, for an expedition to Lancashire
and Yorkshire to report on conditions among the industrial working class. This
eventually appeared as The Road to Wigan Pier. Taylor notes that there were
quite a few writers doing very similar work at the time. One wit commented
that one could barely get any work done down a mine in those days for
middle-class scribblers getting in the way. On his return south, Orwell married
Eileen O'Shaughnessy and the couple moved into a tiny ancient cottage called
‘The Stores’ in Wallington, Hertfordshire, where they ran a loss-making village
store. The ceilings were so low that there was nowhere inside the house where
the 6ft4 Orwell could stand up straight.

The Blairs had only been settled in Wallington for a short time before Orwell
left for Spain and the Spanish Civil War. In Barcelona, and thanks to his
contacts with the Independent Labour Party, he signed up to fight with the POUM
militia. Eileen soon followed and while Orwell was at the front, she worked for
the ILP offices there. Taylor speculates that she may have had a relationship
with Orwell’s Belgian commander Georges Kopp. Orwell’s time in Spain is well
documented in Homage to Catalonia, which I’ve already read and reviewed, so I
won’t say any more about it here. Orwell had never been a well man and the
Spanish expedition marked a severe downturn in his health. His tuberculosis got
significantly worse, resulting in a long hospital stay after his return to
England. After he was discharged an anonymous benefactor paid for him and
Eileen to recuperate in Marrakech. Orwell, a lover of wet, green England, didn’t
enjoy Morocco. He found the Arabs repulsive, and French colonial rule
oppressive. It was quite a productive time though; he wrote most of Coming Up
for Air, his final pre-war novel, while he was there. It’s interesting that,
while Orwell is always seen as a political writer, he didn’t really have any
strongly held political associations until after the Spanish Civil War when he
declared himself an avowed socialist. His stance was strongly pacifist and
anti-war until almost the outbreak, but in a Damascene conversion he U-turned
and declared himself ready to fight against Hitler and the Nazis.

Orwell tried to join the armed forces at the outbreak of war, but was thwarted
by his ill-health. His disappointment was somewhat alleviated when he became an
active and enthusiastic member of the Home Guard; at one point writing that it
could become a revolutionary people’s militia, an idea that didn’t survive
contact with reality. He worked for the BBC’s propaganda department, where he
formulated many of the ideas that would surface in 1984. He became a fixture of
literary London, getting to know many of the big names of the time, including
H.G. Wells, whom he told to his face that his best work had all been done prior
to WWI! At the end of the war he was sent by the Observer as a war
correspondent to newly liberated Europe, travelling first to Paris and then on
to Germany. While he was away, his wife Eileen died in London during an operation,
leaving Orwell solely responsible for their recently adopted baby boy, Richard.

In the immediate post-war years he lived as a single father in Dickensian
conditions in London. He hired a housekeeper and nanny who helped look after
Richard. It was the publication of Animal Farm that finally made Orwell an
unambiguously successful author and able to live comfortably on the proceeds
from book sales. With this new freedom from having to supplement his income
with journalism, he finally realised his dream of retiring to a remote Scottish
island to dedicate himself to writing. At Barnhill on the Isle of Jura in the
Hebrides, with his sister and adopted son, he lived a primitive existence far
from the literary London world he’d inhabited through the war years. It was
here that he wrote 1984. All the while his health declined until he was
admitted to a succession of sanatoriums. With the publication of 1984, his
masterpiece, Orwell entered the last year of his life. On the one hand he was
rich, on the other he was too ill to enjoy it. He had a bizarre death-bed
marriage to Sonia Brownell, surely an epic bit of last minute literary gold
digging on her part. In her favour, she did seem to do her best to manage
Orwell’s legacy responsibly.

Orwell was one of those rare individuals, wholly immune to any need to join a
group or fit-in. The kind of man who could happily raise his hand in a room
full of people all convinced they are in agreement, and disagree. While a
committed socialist, he refused to acquiesce in the British left’s reverence
for the Soviet Union. The craven worship of that murderous regime has forever
tarnished the reputation of the mid-century intellectual left. He was able to
tell it as he saw it, and with his intelligence and insight, he often saw
further and deeper than any other political commentator of the time. His
description of totalitarianism was expert and timely. 1984 has probably done
more than any other work to inoculate the west against totalitarian communism;
we still use “Orwellian” to describe any restriction of freedom of thought or
slide towards overt infringement on people’s freedom.

Taylor has done a superb job bringing Orwell to life. I read a review of this
book that said, “never meet your heroes”, as if the recently unearthed details
of Orwell’s rather inept attempts at courtship, and his occasional neglect of
his wife in some way tarnish his reputation. I didn’t feel this at all. It was
a privilege to get to know this genius better. The faults simply made him more
human to me. I don’t get the modern requirement that great thinkers and artists
must also be saints. Having read a large portion of his interwar reportage I am
a bigger fan than ever. You don’t have to agree with everything he wrote,
indeed he didn’t agree with everything he wrote, to appreciate one of the
world’s most original political thinkers, and one whom we still refer to
nearly 80 years later.
