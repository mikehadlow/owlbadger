I do like a handsome building; I think everyone does. Architecture directly
affects our daily experience of life like no other art. I expect most people
have strong opinions about what they like in a building and what they dislike.
I certainly do. Yet, despite having those strong opinions, I’ve very rarely
read a book about architecture, and if you asked me to count off some famous
architects I doubt I’d exhaust the fingers of one hand. With my elderly Mum in
a nursing home in Hastings, I’ve found myself regularly stopping off for lunch
on my fortnightly visits at the De La Warr Pavilion in Bexhill. It’s proclaimed
as a modernist masterpiece; it certainly is a very impressive building; the
circular staircase alone is worth making a trip to see. It was built in the
1930s and must have appeared as a spaceship on the promenade of the otherwise
quite dowdy Bexhill. One of the delights of a visit there is the excellent
bookshop, with its collection of works on architecture and design. It was while
browsing the shelves that I first came across this book. I must have picked it
up half a dozen times before deciding to buy a copy. I decided that I needed an
education in architecture, and what better way to start than to fit this into
my interwar reading list. It was published posthumously; the author, Gavin
Stamp, had died in 2017 leaving the unfinished manuscript. His wife organised
its publication and it was finally released in 2024. Stamp was a prolific
journalist and architectural historian, and a founder and the first chairman of
the Thirties Society, now the Twentieth Century Society, which aims to preserve
notable buildings of the period. He was apparently one of the leading
campaigners who saved both Battersea and Bankside power stations; the latter is
now the Tate Modern.

The book leads with the immediate postwar construction of tens of thousands of
war memorials across the country. Some are merely a stone cross in a
churchyard, or a plaque, but almost every village in the country has one. Many
however are significant architectural and sculptural creations. Here in Lewes
we have a very attractive plinth with bronze statues in a commanding position
at the top of School Hill, designed by Vernon March, who is also responsible
for the national war memorial of Canada in Ottawa. They mostly adopted a
classical style, with Lutyens’s influence to the fore. Lutyens’s own work,
especially the Thiepval Memorial to the missing of the Somme, Stamp considers
to be the greatest work of 20th-century architecture. He also lavishes praise
on the Royal Artillery Memorial at Hyde Park Corner, sculpted by Charles
Sargeant Jagger. Stamp is rather dismissive about the Cardiff memorial that we
know well from my son Leo’s two graduation ceremonies, but I rather like it.
It’s certainly popular with graduating students and their families as their
favourite photographic backdrop.

British architecture had been self-contained, or perhaps self-confident, in the
Edwardian period, but post-WWI there was an increasing interest in recent
Swedish and Dutch buildings. It seems that Northern Europeans had taken the
English Arts and Crafts ideas and developed them, while in Britain they had
never progressed much beyond domestic architecture. Much of what we think of as
typical 30s architecture: those large unbroken surfaces of brick, horizontal
features, steel window frames, and simple decoration can be traced back to the
Scandinavians. Norwich City Hall, which I know well from my days as a
university student, is cited as the epitome of this style. I’m not a fan. It
lacks the grace of classical architecture, but without the charm of the
original Arts and Crafts style.

Probably the most successful aesthetic development of the interwar period was
what we now call Art Deco. It is mostly a product of French and American
influence with a thorough injection of ancient Egyptian following Howard
Carter’s sensational discovery of Tutankhamun’s tomb and its treasures.
American influence also introduced new standards of luxury: en-suite bathrooms,
extensive and creative electric lighting, and central heating. British
architects were fascinated by American skyscrapers and attempted to imitate
their aesthetic, despite building codes in London keeping roof heights below
80ft. Deco was itself a development of Classicism, but the purest classical
style of the period was Neo-Georgian, which had become established, especially
for public buildings, in the Edwardian period. Lutyens is probably the purest
example of this. Stamp is a fan and devotes space to Lutyens’s masterpiece, the
government quarter of New Delhi, despite it not being in Britain.

The most startling statistic in the book is the number of new houses that were
built in the interwar period, around four million. Of these only around 50 were
modernist designs; the vast majority were the much despised “mock-Tudor”, with
fake beams stuck to the front of a modern brick house. The architectural press
at the time was scathing about it, and has remained so ever since. Stamp is
critical of the profession’s condescension towards the jobbing builder’s love
of mock-Tudor. He points out that the so-called jerry-built suburbs of Tudor
houses built during the interwar period have not turned into slums as predicted
by the critics, but have remained on the whole desirable suburbs. Probably
because they were what people wanted and catered to basic human desires for
domestic comfort and cosiness. They are far more successful than the equivalent
modernist blocks built later in the 60s and 70s, which really have turned into
slums. To my mind there was a missed opportunity here to embrace the
“Tudorbethan”, merge it with Arts and Crafts and create a truly English
domestic vernacular, but because the architectural profession so despised it,
they let it slip from their fingers.

The most important architectural development of the interwar period was the
emergence of the “modern” style. Under the influence of Le Corbusier and the
Bauhaus it celebrated a minimalist aesthetic of concrete, flat roofs, and plain
lines devoid of decoration. To my eyes there’s also a strong Japanese
influence, especially in some of the glass and steel domestic architecture. The
modernist movement was simultaneously a reaction to the trauma of the Great
War, the old Edwardian attitudes that had accompanied it, and an embrace of
modern ideas about exercise, fitness, and the importance of sunshine and fresh
air. It soon became apparent though that reinforced concrete, single-pane metal
windows, and easily corroded steel were not suitable materials for the English
climate. The clean white modernist forms looked horrible when streaked with
damp and rust after a few years. The flat roof was almost an ideological tenet
of the modernists, but it too was simply unsuitable for the constant rain of an
English winter. A new modernist architecture using traditional materials like
brick, tiles, and stone began to emerge by the end of the 30s, but to my eyes
these buildings aren’t much of an improvement over the rusting concrete ones.

Stamp writes engagingly. I mostly enjoyed the book, but it’s a daunting place
to start, especially for a layman like me. The parade of buildings and
architects was overwhelming at times. I admit I even skipped over some
sections, especially in the chapter on modern church architecture, which is
uniformly awful. What emerges from the book is the story of an aesthetic
profession – architecture – that parted company with the general public over
what a building should look like. Of course it was part of a broad modernist
movement that marched in parallel across the arts. The difference between
architecture and the rest is that the general public neither choose the design
of buildings, nor have the choice of buying something else if they don’t like
them. Modernist art, music, and literature, marched off into irrelevant
academic echo chambers, while commercial arts flourished in their absence. Some
arts simply committed suicide – poetry, for example. Unfortunately for us
ordinary people, we have to suffer the aesthetic crimes of the modernist
architects, and some poor condemned souls even have to live in them. It’s
astonishing that a century later architects still haven’t woken from their
self-induced hallucination that what they create is in any way attractive. It
will probably take a cultural turning point of some kind that at last rejects
modernism in all its forms before we return to creating buildings that people
actually like.
