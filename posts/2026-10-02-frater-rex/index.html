Rex Whistler

The Artist and His Patrons

By Nikki Frater

My Dad introduced Rex Whistler to me. He was a well-known interwar muralist and would have been a fixture of my Dad’s art school education in the late 1940s, despite having died in WWII. I once tried to read his brother, Laurence Whistler’s, biography but found it so badly written that I gave up after a couple of chapters. Why does art biography attract such poor writers? With this year’s theme of interwar Britain, Whistler kept turning up. First, in February, on a visit to Tate Britain to see the Lee Miller exhibition, I stumbled across his massive early mural in the old dining room. It’s famously controversial because of its racist depiction of black people. This is what I wrote in my diary:

While eating lunch I’d noticed a sign for the Rex Whistler mural. I’m quite a fan so after exiting the Lee Miller show I went back downstairs to take a look. Whistler had painted the mural as a young 22-year-old student in the mid 1920s, almost exactly 100 years ago. It had been part of the cafe’s dining hall, but now the room had been dedicated to the mural. In the centre hung two cinema projection screens and a film about the mural was being shown on a loop. I sat down to watch. What transpired had all the trappings of a Maoist “struggle session”, its intention was obviously to examine the racist imagery in the mural, but its effect was an exhibit of all that is so wrong with the woke movement. One actor played a middle-aged female art historian, the inquisitor, the other played the young Rex Whistler, the victim. She subjected him to a barrage of questions accusing him of class elitism and gross racism. It was all very disturbing. Even his death as a tank commander in WWII fighting real Nazis, not imaginary ones, was twisted into a kind of racist militarism. I was astounded by the lack of self-awareness. Maybe in another 100 years’ time this film will be an exhibit in an exhibition about the west’s equivalent of Mao’s cultural revolution?

One can well imagine the agonising discussions amongst the Tate’s management over the mural. Do they destroy it and be accused of being no better than the Taliban, blowing up Buddhist statues, or do they leave it and be accused of promoting racist imagery? I suppose the ridiculously awful film is the least bad compromise?

Whistler’s next appearance was in Chips Channon’s diaries, which have been my bedtime read for much of the year. Whistler was a fixture of the “bright young things”, the aristocratic set that intersected frequently with Channon’s circle. Whistler painted murals for Channon’s luxuriously appointed London house, 5 Belgrave Square, and they remained friends. Channon described him as “fawn like”, “vague”, but also charming. After Whistler joined the army he often stayed at Belgrave Square on leave. Whistler also appeared in Adrian Tinniswood’s The Long Weekend, which I read recently. As a friend of Cecil Beaton and the most fashionable muralist of the interwar period, he was responsible for decorating many of the houses described in the book.

I can’t remember how this book came to my attention, but once it did I had to add it to my interwar reading list. It makes a great addition to Adrian Tinniswood’s book, covering many of the same characters and their houses, but focussing entirely on Whistler and his work. It’s a large format paperback, typical of many art books. Many of the surviving murals are given full page spreads, often with accompanying enlargements of various details, which makes it a visual joy to read. Each chapter details Whistler’s work for a different client in a vaguely chronological order. Nikki Frater thankfully doesn’t suffer from the art writer’s curse, and writes attractive and fluent prose. She nods to the woke criticism of Whistler’s sometimes racist imagery, but then thankfully moves on.

Frater makes a brave attempt at Whistler’s sex life, but it seems he’s difficult to pin down. Was he gay? He certainly moved in very gay circles and had an intimate relationship with a gay man, Malcolm Bullock, but there’s no indication that it was ever physical. What is clear is that later in the ’30s he was romantically involved with a succession of aristocratic women, especially the love of his life, Caroline Paget. He never married or settled down with any man or woman and died young. Frater describes his love life as romantic and hopeless.

As an artist he was a technical virtuoso. He seemed little interested in the prevailing fashions of the 20s and 30s, preferring a romantic, slightly cartoonish, realism. He was apparently a very fast worker, an asset when you have vast aristocratic walls to cover. I’ve only seen a few of his works in the flesh: one a cartoon of George IV in the Brighton Pavilion, painted when he was stationed there during the war; the large mural covering all the walls of a room in Philip Sassoon’s house, Port Lympne; and of course the massive Tate mural. I’m now determined to see the Plas Newydd murals that are considered his masterpiece. I’m already planning a trip to Wales and Anglesey next spring.

At the outbreak of WWII Whistler joined the Welsh Guards, despite being rather old at age 34. He believed that artists too should play their part. He trained as a tank commander, with Chips Channon commenting that he couldn’t imagine a more unwarlike warrior. He continued to paint in the army, including some very good portraits of fellow officers and army life. He was tragically killed by a mortar shell in Normandy on the 18th July 1944 during Operation Goodwood; the attempt by the British army to take Caen. He was 39. One wonders what he would have done had he survived? There would probably have been some excellent war paintings. Would the experience of war have influenced his style and subject matter? Frater speculates that he would have found a perfect outlet in the burgeoning TV and film business after the war. Or perhaps he would have evolved into a respected gallery artist? But this is all idle speculation, we will never know.

 

Mike Hadlow, Oct 2 2026
