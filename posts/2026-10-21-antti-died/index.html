The Man Who Died

By Antti Tuomainen

This was Bob England’s choice for Trafalgar Street Book Club’s November meeting. The author, Antti Tuomainen, is Finnish, and is apparently regarded as the King of Helsinki Noir. This book, originally written in Tuomainen’s native Finnish, was translated by David Hackston. I hadn’t heard of Tuomainen or Hackston before Bob nominated the book. Originally published in 2016, my copy is a new paperback bought from Amazon. At 245 pages it’s comfortably under the TSBC limit of 300 pages. It was a very easy read and I finished it in four days.

The story is a murder mystery, a genre that I almost never read, with the interesting twist that the detective is also the victim. The book opens with the hero, Jaakko Kaunismaa, a 35-year-old mushroom farmer, being told by his doctor that he’s been poisoned and has but a few weeks to live. Jaakko, in the next chapter, discovers his wife having sex with one of his young employees in their back garden and naturally assumes that it is his wife who is poisoning him. A second possible culprit is revealed when three men from a rival firm show up and threaten him with unspecified consequences if he competes with them. What follows is a rather ramshackle tale as Jaakko drives around discovering various clues, while at the same time dodging attempts by the rival firm’s henchmen to kill him. In doing so they suffer bizarre and deadly accidents; one stabbing himself in the head with a samurai sword and another falling into a sauna’s fire and roasting his head alive. All the while an incompetent and uninterested police officer occasionally questions him. A love story is introduced when he is seduced by another of his employees. It’s all chaotically rambling along when it rather suddenly comes to an abrupt and unsatisfying conclusion. The poisoner is revealed to be one of his staff, who has no discernible motive, while the rival firm meet their comeuppance in a confused standoff in the forest.

I wasn’t sure quite what to make of the book. As I said it’s not a genre that I normally read, so I’ve nothing to compare it with. I don’t think it’s meant to be taken very seriously. Much of the time it’s quite fun; Jaakko motors around having various violent and comic encounters. Tuomainen captures the feel of a small Finnish town in the summer, describing Jaakko’s journeys almost turn by turn. The forests, lakes, and coastline all add to the atmosphere. I felt I could almost find my way around the small town of Hamina after reading it. Indeed, it’s a real place, so I suppose if one was super keen one could find the actual streets where the characters live on Google maps.

Knowing he is going to die gives Jaakko a devil-may-care attitude; it gives the book something of an American Beauty vibe. At times he is also rather philosophical about what it means to live and die, but the philosophical passages don’t really land because the tone of the rest of the book is so frivolous. Tuomainen is also not particularly bothered to explain obvious issues with the narrative. When the first henchman of the rival company stabs himself next to a river, Jaakko just leaves the body there with the henchman’s car still sitting next to the road. Surely someone would notice it? But neither the police nor the public seem overly bothered by the abandoned vehicle. The second henchman dies after attacking Jaakko violently with an axe in his friend’s sauna, but the friend never asks about the axe cuts in the sauna walls or the shallow grave in his garden. However, the book’s greatest failing is its ending. It gives a strong impression of an author bored with his story and in a hurry to end it quickly. It’s a shame because the opening premise — a man investigating his own murder — was quite promising.

The verdict at November’s TSBC was mixed. Like me, several of the others thought it was quite a fun read. One didn’t enjoy it at all though, and said he’d wasted his time reading it. Definitely neither a profound nor particularly satisfying book, and not quite funny enough to make up for it. One of our members suggested it might just be a bad translation, but that wouldn’t explain the awkward and perplexing plot. I guess at best I’d recommend it as a light-hearted holiday read for someone who wanted to get to know Finland better. I suppose if one is a native of Hamina it might even make sense?

 

Mike Hadlow, Oct 21 2026
